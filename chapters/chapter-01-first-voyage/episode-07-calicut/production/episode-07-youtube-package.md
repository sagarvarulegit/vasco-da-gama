In May 1498, after ten grueling months at sea and 12,000 miles of uncharted ocean, Vasco da Gama’s three surviving wooden ships dropped anchor off the trading metropolis of Calicut (Kozhikode), India.

What the Portuguese expected was a primitive land waiting to be discovered. What they found was the glittering center of global commerce—a civilization wealthier, more sophisticated, and far more powerful than 15th-century Portugal.

From being paraded through packed city streets in covered palanquins escorted by hundreds of armed Nair warriors, to mistaking a Hindu temple for a church of the Virgin Mary, Vasco da Gama finally stood before the Samutiri Raja (Zamorin) of Calicut, who reclined on green velvet draped in enormous emeralds and rubies.

But when the Portuguese presented their meager trade goods—cheap brass basins and wool caps—the royal court erupted in mocking laughter. Accused of being pirates by wealthy Arab merchants, Da Gama was detained under armed guard at Pandarani as his fleet offshore prepared their cannons for battle.
